Every country plays football. But which sport does each country play far more than the rest of the world? I counted {{meta.clubs|int}} sports clubs and coloured every country by its signature sport. Pesäpallo, tchoukball, hurling and speedway all have a country of their own.

Maps of "the most popular sport in each country" are nearly all one colour. Football wins almost everywhere, and in the data I used it is {{meta.football_share|pct}} of all the clubs in the world. That map is true and tells you nothing about what makes Finland different from Spain.

So I asked a different question: which sport does each country have more of than you would expect? If a sport is 1% of the world's clubs but 10% of one country's, that country plays it ten times as much as the world average. Geographers call this a location quotient. It has a lovely property for messy open data: because it compares each country with itself, it does not matter that Germany has {{countries.DEU.clubs|int}} clubs recorded and Nigeria {{countries.NGA.clubs|int}}.

A map of national quirks

Of the {{signature.n_countries_mapped|int}} countries with at least {{thr.country|int}} clubs in Wikidata, {{signature.n_signature|int}} have a clear signature sport, and {{signature.n_sports|int}} different sports win somewhere. Some are exactly what you would guess: cricket in Sri Lanka, Pakistan, India and Bangladesh, Australian rules football in Australia ({{sig.AUS.lq|x}} the world average), Canadian football in Canada ({{sig.CAN.lq|x}}), hurling in Ireland ({{sig.IRL.lq|x}}).

Others were news to me. Finland's signature is pesäpallo, its own fast, zig-zagging version of baseball, at {{sig.FIN.lq|x}} the world average; every pesäpallo club in the data is Finnish. Switzerland's is tchoukball, a no-contact game played by bouncing a ball off a small rebound frame, devised in Switzerland around 1970. Poland's is speedway, motorbikes with no brakes sliding round shale ovals ({{sig.POL.lq|x}}). The Netherlands' is korfball ({{sig.NLD.lq|x}}), the mixed team game first played in Amsterdam in 1902.

Zoom out and blocks appear. Handball is the signature in a band from Tunisia through Romania to the former Yugoslavia. Volleyball rules from Turkey to Kazakhstan. Futsal is the signature in Brazil, Iran, South Korea and Ukraine. Cycling takes the Andes: Colombia, Ecuador and Venezuela.

Small numbers, big ratios

There is a trap in ratios like these. One croquet club in a small country can make it look like the croquet capital of the world. So I set three rules. A country needs at least {{thr.country|int}} clubs to be mapped at all. A sport needs at least {{thr.cell|int}} clubs in a country before that country can be its heartland. And the winner is picked with a cautious version of the ratio, which pretends each count is a bit smaller than it is: the lower end of a 90% range for it. Ten clubs count as about {{thr.lo10|f1}}; a hundred count as about {{thr.lo100|f1}}. A small sport has to lead by a mile to win.

Pick a sport

The interactive map lets you choose any of {{meta.sports_listed|int}} sports and see where it glows. Bandy, hockey with a ball on an ice rink the size of a football pitch, belongs to Sweden ({{top.bandy.0.lq|x}}), Russia and Finland. Field hockey's heartland is the Netherlands ({{top.field hockey.0.lq|x}}), then India and Argentina. Water polo's is Malta, Hungary and Italy. Where clubs have coordinates you can go inside countries too: rowing is Spain's signature sport, and it turns out to be a Basque affair, with Gipuzkoa at {{region_top.rowing.0.lq|x}} the world average, home of the trainera boat races. Hurling peaks in Tipperary ({{region_top.hurling and camogie.0.lq|x}}).

Where the data fools you

Wikidata is built by volunteers, and sometimes by bulk imports of whole registries. The location quotient cancels out how much of a country is recorded, but not which sports. The United Kingdom's signature comes out as target shooting, because someone added {{caveat.uk_shooting|int}} British target shooting clubs with near-identical descriptions. Sweden's comes out as minigolf for the same reason.

So the page has a switch that counts only the {{wp.clubs|int}} clubs with a Wikipedia article. That removes most imports, though it has its own bias towards famous clubs. {{wp.same_sig|int}} of {{wp.both|int}} countries keep the same signature either way. The UK becomes cricket, Sweden orienteering, Hungary water polo, France rugby union. The heartlands that survive both versions, pesäpallo, hurling, speedway, korfball, bandy and cricket, are the ones I would bet on.

Do medals agree?

Only loosely. I compared the club figures with all-time Olympic medal tables for {{medals.articles|int}} sports. Across {{medals.pairs|int}} country and sport pairs the rank correlation is {{medals.spearman|f2}}: medals reward a few elite athletes, clubs measure everyday play. The pairs that are high on both are the real heartlands: Finland in wrestling, Canada in curling, Norway in skiing, Hungary in water polo and Colombia in cycling. Hungary has more Olympic water polo medals than any other country, and water polo is its signature sport once the imports are filtered out.

How I did this

I took every Wikidata item that is a sports club or team with a sport and a country ({{meta.wikidata_items_all|int}} items), removed national sides, Olympic delegations, team seasons and school and college teams, merged variants of each sport and split clubs listed under several sports so each counts once. That left {{meta.clubs|int}} clubs. For each sport and country I divided the sport's share of the country's clubs by its share of the world's, and picked each country's signature sport with the cautious ratio described above. Regions use clubs with coordinates ({{meta.located_share|pct}} of them). The UK map does the same with OpenStreetMap places to play against the UK mix. These are counts of organised clubs as recorded, not of players, and every number is in the data files linked below.
